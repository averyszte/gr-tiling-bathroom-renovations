## INLINE PROSE SECTION 1

### What actually makes a wet room a wet room

People assume a wet room is just a shower without the tray. On site it is the opposite — it is the most demanding waterproofing job in domestic tiling, and everything you see is riding on a system you never will. The tiles and grout are a wearing surface, not a waterproof layer. Cement grout is porous, and every single joint is a potential path for water. What actually keeps the room dry is a continuous tanking membrane hidden beneath the tile, bonded to walls and floor and sealed at every junction, pipe and drain before a single tile goes down.

That is where the skill and the risk live. Most shower leaks are not a failure of the flat field of tiling — they are a detailing failure at a corner, a floor-to-wall junction, or an unsealed pipe penetration. We tank the whole wet zone as one tank: primer and flexible liquid membrane, or a bonded sheet system, with reinforcing tape banding every internal corner and every point where the waterproofing changes plane. Pipe collars and the shower waste are sealed into the membrane, not just caulked around afterwards. The membrane is carried high enough up the wall that splashing and steam never reach an unprotected substrate. And crucially, we let the tanking fully cure before we tile — rushing that step is one of the most common reasons a beautiful wet room fails within a year.

The pay-off for getting this right is a room that is genuinely open, easy to clean, and impossible to date. The consequence of getting it wrong is not a repair — it is a rebuild. If you want the wider fit-out managed end to end, that sits with our [full bathroom renovations](/services/bathroom-renovations); this page is about the waterproofing craft that makes a wet room safe to build in the first place.

- Continuous tanking across floor and walls to full wet-zone height
- Reinforcing tape banding every corner, junction and change of plane
- Pipe penetrations and shower waste sealed into the membrane, not caulked over
- Compatible primer, membrane and adhesive from one system — never mixed brands
- Tanking fully cured and checked before any tile is fixed
- Independent flood/soak check on the finished detail where the build allows

## INLINE PROSE SECTION 2

### Falls, drainage and slip-resistance — the parts you feel underfoot

A wet room has no upstand to contain water, so the floor itself has to do the containing. That means building a gradient — a gentle, even fall across the whole shower area toward the drain — so water always runs away rather than spreading across the room or pooling in a low spot. Get the fall too flat and you get standing water and staining; too steep and it becomes uncomfortable and unsafe underfoot. On a former (the pre-graded shower base) this is engineered in; on a screeded floor we form the falls by hand, and setting them correctly to either a linear channel drain or a central point drain is one of the genuinely skilled parts of the job.

The tile spec on that floor is not a style choice, it is a safety one. Wet-room floors need grip when wet, so we specify slip-resistant porcelain — R11 on the DIN 51130 ramp scale is our sensible minimum for a frequently and unpredictably wet floor, well above the R10 you might accept on a bathroom used with a mat. Smaller-format tiles or mosaics on the shower floor earn their keep twice over: the extra grout lines add grip, and they let the floor follow the falls to the drain without lipping. The walls are where you get to be ambitious — large-format porcelain with minimal grout lines for a clean, continuous, easy-to-wipe finish.

If you are still deciding between a full wet room and a walk-in shower enclosure within a standard bathroom, that is worth an honest conversation before anything is ordered — see our [bathroom & shower tiling](/services/bathroom-tiling-dublin) page for the enclosure route. And if the driver is mobility or a grant-funded adaptation rather than design, our [accessible & mobility bathrooms](/services/accessible-bathroom-dublin) service is built specifically around that.

- Even, continuous falls formed to a linear or point drain — no ponding
- R11-rated slip-resistant porcelain as the wet-room floor standard
- Small-format or mosaic shower floors for grip and to follow the falls
- Large-format porcelain walls for a clean, low-grout, easy-clean finish
- Drain type chosen around your layout, screed depth and preferred look

## OPTIONAL DEEP SECTION

### Upstairs wet rooms in older Dublin homes — the risk nobody mentions in a showroom

The single biggest wet-room risk in Dublin housing stock is a first-floor wet room built over a suspended timber floor — and that describes a huge share of the Victorian and Edwardian red-brick terraces across Rathmines, Ranelagh, Portobello, Drumcondra and the coastal villages, as well as the semi-detached homes of Cabra, Crumlin, Marino and Raheny. The bathroom often sits in the return, the coldest and dampest part of the house, on joists that decades of plumbers have notched and drilled. Timber moves. Tile does not. Lay rigid tile straight over a flexing timber deck and the floor will crack the tile or the grout, open a path for water, and — in an upstairs wet room — send that water straight into the ceiling below.

You cannot solve this with adhesive alone. A flexible S1 or S2 adhesive helps, but no adhesive absorbs genuine structural deflection. The deck has to be stiffened first — over-boarding, additional noggins — and then decoupled with a matted membrane bonded down over it, so any residual board movement is broken before it ever reaches the tile. Many decoupling mats are also waterproof, which means over a suspended timber wet-room floor they can do two jobs at once: isolate movement and form part of the tank. That is exactly the detail we want in a Dublin return bathroom.

This is also why the cheapest quote is so often the most expensive in the end. A tanking failure over a habitable room is in a different league to a simple regrout — putting right rot, blown plaster, corroded fixings and a damaged ceiling below costs many multiples of what the correct tanking and decoupling would have cost at the start. The quotes that come in suspiciously low are usually the ones skipping the membrane, the priming, the decoupling and the movement joints — the invisible work that is the entire point of a wet room.

Older Dublin floors bring one more consideration for level access: achieving a genuinely flush, barrier-free entry can mean recessing the former or drain into the joist zone, which needs to be assessed against the existing structure. We survey all of this before we quote, so there are no surprises mid-build. For a full picture of what drives the figure up or down, see our guide to [wet room costs](/cost-guide) — we never publish a fixed price, because an honest wet-room quote can only be written after we have seen your floor.

## PROCESS

1. **Survey & Falls Plan** — We assess your floor structure, subfloor and drainage options, then set the level-access design and where the falls run.
2. **Structure & Decouple** — We stiffen any timber deck, install backer boards and bond down a decoupling membrane so movement never reaches the tile.
3. **Tank & Cure** — We apply the full tanking system, band every junction and pipe, seal the drain in, and let it fully cure before tiling begins.
4. **Tile, Grout & Check** — We tile to the falls in R11 floor porcelain, grout and silicone the movement junctions, then flood-check the finished detail.

## FAQ

**Will a wet room leak?**
Not when the waterproofing is done properly. The reason wet rooms leak is almost never the tiles — it is an unsealed junction, corner or pipe penetration behind them. We tank the whole wet zone as one continuous system, band every internal corner and change of plane with reinforcing tape, seal the shower waste and pipes directly into the membrane, and let it fully cure before tiling. Done that way, the tanking, not the grout, keeps the room dry.

**What is tanking and why does it cost extra?**
Tanking is the hidden waterproof membrane — liquid-applied or a bonded sheet — that turns the floor and walls into a sealed tank beneath the tile. It is the extra cost, and the extra time, that separates a wet room from a simple shower. Tiles and cement grout are porous and not waterproof on their own, so without tanking, water gets behind the finish. It is the one part of the job that is never worth saving money on.

**Wet room or walk-in shower — which should I choose?**
A walk-in shower sits within a standard bathroom, usually with a low-profile tray and a glass screen. A true wet room has no tray and no threshold — the whole floor is tanked and graded to a drain. Wet rooms feel more open, are easier to clean and suit small or awkward rooms well, but they cost more because of the drainage, graded screed and full tanking. We will give you an honest steer for your specific room before anything is ordered.

**Can you build a wet room upstairs on a timber floor?**
Yes, and it is one of the most common Dublin jobs we do, but it is also the highest-risk detail in domestic tiling. A timber floor flexes, and that movement will crack rigid tile and open a leak straight into the ceiling below. We stiffen the deck, then bond down a decoupling membrane — often one that is waterproof too — so movement is isolated and the floor forms part of the tank. It has to be surveyed properly first.

**How do you stop the water spreading across the floor?**
With falls. The whole shower area is graded at a gentle, even gradient toward the drain, so water always runs to the outlet rather than travelling across the room. On a pre-formed base this is built in; on a screeded floor we form the falls by hand to a linear channel or a central point drain. Setting that gradient correctly — enough to drain, gentle enough to stay safe underfoot — is one of the most skilled parts of the build.

**Which tiles are safe for a wet-room floor?**
Slip-resistant porcelain. We work to R11 on the DIN 51130 ramp scale as the sensible minimum for a wet-room floor, which is grippier than the R10 you might accept on a bathroom used with a bath mat. Smaller-format tiles or mosaics on the shower floor are ideal because the extra grout lines add grip and let the floor follow the falls to the drain. Walls can be large-format porcelain for a clean, continuous, low-maintenance finish.

**How much does a wet room cost in Dublin?**
It depends on the room, so we only ever give a written quote after a survey. The figure is driven by your floor structure, whether an upstairs timber deck needs stiffening and decoupling, the drainage and screed build-up, the tile spec, and any hidden damp found on strip-out. A wet room carries a genuine premium over a standard shower because of the tanking, falls and drainage. Our [wet room costs](/cost-guide) guide breaks down exactly what moves the price.

**Do you handle grab rails, seats and grant paperwork?**
Those belong to a different service. This page is about the design and waterproofing craft of a wet room. If the project is about mobility, ageing in place, or a Housing Adaptation Grant, our [accessible & mobility bathrooms](/services/accessible-bathroom-dublin) service is built around exactly that — level access, structural pattressing for grab rails, seats and grant-compliant design. A wet room and an accessible bathroom share vocabulary but are quite different briefs, and we will point you to the right one.
