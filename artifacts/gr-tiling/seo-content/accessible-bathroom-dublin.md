## INLINE PROSE SECTION 1

### Bathrooms that keep people safe and independent at home

Most of the calls we get about **accessible bathrooms in Dublin** come from a son, a daughter or a carer rather than the person who'll use the room. Someone has had a fall, or a hospital discharge has made the old over-bath shower a daily risk, and the family wants a bathroom that lets a parent stay in their own home. That is exactly the work we do: turning a standard, hazardous bathroom into a level-access, slip-safe space designed around the person who uses it.

The heart of an accessible bathroom is the **level-access shower** — no tray, no lip, no step to catch a toe or a wheel. The floor is laid to a gentle, controlled fall so water runs to a linear or point drain instead of spreading across the room, and the whole wet zone is finished in **R11-rated non-slip tile** that grips underfoot even when wet. Around that we add the details that make the difference day to day: sturdy grab rails set at the right height for the user, a wall-mounted folding seat, a wall-hung basin that leaves clear space beneath for a wheelchair or a carer, and a half-height shower door that keeps splash in while letting someone assist from outside. If the design calls for a full open wet-floor layout, that's a specialist waterproofing job — see [how we build a level-access wet room](/services/wet-room-installation-dublin) for the tanking and falls craft behind it.

- Step-free, level-access shower with a controlled fall to drain
- R11 non-slip floor tile rated for frequently-wet areas
- Grab rails fixed into solid timber pattressing, never plasterboard plugs
- Wall-mounted basins and folding shower seats for clear, usable space
- Half-height doors for carer access and reduced flooding

## INLINE PROSE SECTION 2

### Grants, paperwork and grant-compliant design

For most Dublin families, the grant is what makes the project possible, so it's worth knowing what's out there. The three main supports are the **Housing Adaptation Grant for People with a Disability** (for changes like level-access showers and adaptations tied to a disability), the **Mobility Aids Grant** (a smaller, faster scheme for basic items such as grab rails and accessible showers), and **Housing Aid for Older Persons** (repairs and improvements for older homeowners). These are administered locally, so which council office you deal with depends on where you live — Dublin City Council, Fingal, Dún Laoghaire-Rathdown or South Dublin County Council — and each runs its own application and approval process.

We're not here to replace your occupational therapist or the council, but we make the contractor side straightforward. We provide the clear, itemised **written quote** these applications need, design the room so it meets the accessibility standards the grant expects, and work to the drawings and recommendations set out by the OT assessment. Because grant funding is means-tested and the amounts and percentages change, we don't quote grant figures on this page — for current bands and what a project typically costs, see our [accessible bathroom costs & grants](/cost-guide) guide. If the adaptation is part of a wider refit — new layout, plumbing moves, the lot — that's better handled as one of our [full bathroom renovations](/services/bathroom-renovations) so the accessible features are designed in from the start rather than bolted on.

## OPTIONAL DEEP SECTION

### Why grab rails and level entry are a trade skill, not an add-on

A grab rail is only as safe as what it's screwed into, and this is where cut-price accessible jobs quietly fail. In a lot of Dublin homes — the 1930s–70s semis in Cabra, Crumlin, Marino and Raheny especially — bathroom walls are **timber stud rather than solid block**. You cannot reliably hang a load-bearing grab rail off plasterboard and plastic plugs; lean on it in an emergency and it can tear straight out of the board. The correct method is to install a **timber pattress — a plywood or noggin backing behind the plasterboard — before the wall is tiled**, so the rail is bolted into something structural. In solid masonry, older red-brick terraces in Rathmines or Phibsborough for instance, we use appropriate resin or expansion fixings instead. Either way, the safe fixing has to be planned in before tiling, not discovered afterwards.

Level entry is the other detail that looks simple and isn't. Getting rid of the step means the shower floor has to fall to the drain at just the right gradient — enough that water always drains and never pools, gentle enough that it's safe underfoot and passable for a wheelchair. Too flat and you get standing water; too steep and you've created the trip-and-roll hazard you were trying to remove. On the suspended timber floors common in Dublin's older upstairs bathrooms, that fall has to be formed into a stiffened, decoupled and waterproofed deck so the structure can't flex and crack the tile. It's careful, standards-led work — and it's the reason we won't cut corners on preparation to shave a quote.

## FAQ (7 Q&A)

**Q1. What grants are available for an accessible bathroom in Dublin?**
The three main supports are the Housing Adaptation Grant for People with a Disability, the Mobility Aids Grant for smaller works like grab rails and accessible showers, and Housing Aid for Older Persons. They're administered by your local authority — Dublin City, Fingal, Dún Laoghaire-Rathdown or South Dublin — and are means-tested. We supply the itemised written quote applications need. Current amounts and bands are on our cost guide.

**Q2. Do you handle the grant paperwork for me?**
We handle the contractor's part properly: a clear, itemised written quote, a design that meets the accessibility standards the grant expects, and work carried out to your occupational therapist's recommendations. The application itself, the means test and the OT assessment go through the council and HSE, so those stay with you. We've worked alongside plenty of Dublin families through this process and will give you exactly the documentation the council asks a contractor to provide.

**Q3. What makes a shower genuinely "level-access"?**
There's no tray and no lip — the floor is continuous and step-free, laid to a gentle, controlled fall so water runs to a linear or point drain rather than spreading. The wet zone is finished in R11-rated non-slip tile, and we usually add a wall-fixed folding seat and a half-height door. Getting the gradient right is the skilled part: enough to drain fully, gentle enough to be safe and wheelchair-friendly.

**Q4. Can grab rails go on any bathroom wall?**
Not safely. Many Dublin homes have timber stud walls, and a load-bearing rail fixed only into plasterboard can tear out under weight. The correct method is to install a timber pattress — a plywood or noggin backing — behind the board before tiling, so the rail bolts into something structural. In solid masonry we use resin or expansion fixings. This has to be planned before tiling, which is why we design rail positions in from the start.

**Q5. How long does an accessible bathroom take to install?**
A focused accessible conversion typically takes around three to five days on site, depending on the condition of the existing room and whether we uncover damp, rot or a floor that needs stiffening once we strip out. If the work is part of a wider refit with plumbing moves and a new layout, it runs longer and is better treated as a full renovation. We give you a realistic timeline with your written quote.

**Q6. What does an accessible bathroom cost?**
Cost depends on the room: whether the floor needs stiffening, whether we find hidden damp, the drainage build-up for level access, tile choice and how much sanitaryware changes. Grant funding also affects what you pay out of pocket. We never publish fixed prices — every job gets an honest, itemised written quote after we've seen the space. For typical ranges and how grants offset them, see our accessible bathroom costs and grants guide.

**Q7. What's the difference between an accessible bathroom and a wet room?**
They overlap but the reason to build differs. An accessible bathroom is driven by mobility and safety — level entry, grab rails, seating, non-slip floors and grant-compliant design. A wet room is driven by design and the waterproofing craft of a fully open, tanked space. We build both, and many accessible projects are level-access wet rooms; the tanking and falls detail is covered on our wet room installation page.
