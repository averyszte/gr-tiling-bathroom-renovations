## INLINE PROSE SECTION 1

### Floor and wall tiling in Dublin, done on a substrate that will hold it

Most tiling problems we are called out to fix in Dublin have nothing to do with the tile. The tile is fine. What failed is underneath it — a bouncy timber floor that was never stiffened, a wall skimmed in old gypsum that the adhesive never gripped, a floor 20mm out of level that left large-format tiles lipping at every edge. Getting **floor and wall tiling Dublin** homeowners can rely on means treating preparation as the job, not the warm-up to it.

Every substrate gets read before a single tile is fixed. Absorbent, dusty floors are primed so the adhesive bonds instead of drying out too fast; non-absorbent surfaces get a bonding primer. Floors out by more than a few millimetres are corrected with self-levelling compound over a primed, sound base, or a latex screed where the deviation is bigger. Where you have suspended timber upstairs — standard in the Victorian and Edwardian red-bricks across Rathmines, Ranelagh, Phibsborough and Drumcondra — the deck is stiffened and decoupled before anything rigid goes on top. This is general floor and wall work across the home; for **[bathroom & shower tiling](/services/bathroom-tiling-dublin)** and wet-zone waterproofing, and for **[kitchen tiling & splashbacks](/services/kitchen-tiling-dublin)**, those craft-specific details live on their own pages.

We tile the parts of the house that take daily punishment without being wet rooms — hallways that funnel every muddy shoe in from the street, open-plan living and dining floors, utility rooms and landings. Standard-format porcelain in a straight lay keeps a job efficient; large-format tiles, natural stone and patterns like herringbone are slower, more skilled and worth discussing up front so the quote reflects the real work.

- Substrate surveyed and primed before any tile is set
- Floors levelled with self-levelling compound or latex screed
- Suspended timber floors stiffened and decoupled, not just glued
- Large-format tiles solid-bed fixed and back-buttered, no dot-and-dab
- Perimeter and movement joints planned into the layout from the start
- Setting-out signed off with you before the first tile goes down

## INLINE PROSE SECTION 2

### Choosing the right tile, adhesive and heating for an Irish home

The tile you pick changes how the floor is built and how it lives. **Porcelain** is dense, barely absorbs water and shrugs off frost and stains — the sensible default for a busy Dublin hallway or any threshold that sees weather. **Ceramic** is softer and more absorbent, fine and economical for walls and light-use floors. **Natural stone** — marble, limestone, travertine — is beautiful but porous; it needs sealing, it can etch and stain, and it comes with a maintenance conversation we would rather have before you buy than after.

Adhesive is matched to the substrate, not chosen by habit. Under the EN 12004 standard, an S1 adhesive is deformable and an S2 is highly deformable, and a flexing substrate — suspended timber, a stud wall — needs that flexibility, commonly S2 where movement is real. But no adhesive rescues a genuinely bouncy floor: the deck is stiffened first, then decoupled, then tiled. Movement and expansion joints follow BS 5385 best practice — at floor perimeters, over structural joints, at changes of substrate and around the edges of any heated zone — because omitting them is a leading cause of tented, cracked floors.

Tile is the ideal finish over **underfloor heating**, and in Dublin's older, poorly insulated houses it turns a cold floor warm underfoot. Electric mats suit a single retrofit room; wet systems suit bigger projects. Either way we use a flexible adhesive and flexible grout, commission the heat gradually per the manufacturer's instructions, and keep perimeter movement joints so the assembly can expand and contract without stressing the tile.

- Porcelain, ceramic or stone matched to the room's use and traffic
- EN 12004 S1/S2 flexible adhesives on timber and stud substrates
- BS 5385 movement joints at perimeters, substrate changes and heated edges
- Underfloor heating tiled with flexible adhesive and grout, heated gradually
- Natural stone sealed, with a clear care conversation before you commit
- Honest guidance on **[what drives tiling costs](/cost-guide)** before you commit

## OPTIONAL DEEP SECTION

### Tiling over suspended timber floors in older Dublin homes

The single most common way a floor fails in a Dublin terrace or 1930s semi is being tiled straight onto a suspended timber deck that moves. Tile and grout are rigid; timber joists — often undersized by modern standards, and drilled and notched by decades of plumbers — flex underfoot. That deflection has to go somewhere, and it goes into the weakest line: the grout joint first, then the tile itself, cracking in a straight run across the floor. We see it constantly in return extensions and upstairs rooms across the older stock.

Fixing it properly is a sequence, not a product. First the deck is stiffened — over-boarding, additional noggins, sometimes sistering joists — so the floor stops bouncing. Then a decoupling (uncoupling) membrane is bonded down: a matted layer that separates the tile from the remaining small movements in the timber, so board movement is absorbed instead of transmitted into the finish. Only then does tiling begin, with a flexible S1 or S2 adhesive and flexible grout, and with perimeter movement joints left against every wall. A "waterproof" adhesive or a flexible grout on its own does none of this — flexibility manages micro-movement, it does not carry structural deflection.

Old floors also lie about their level. A 20mm dip across a room is normal in a period house, and it is invisible until large-format tiles start lipping at their edges. That is why levelling and a genuinely flat bed matter more the bigger the tile gets. Where a floor sits in a wet zone rather than a hall or living room, the same decoupling logic applies but the waterproofing detail changes — that is covered on our **[bathroom & shower tiling](/services/bathroom-tiling-dublin)** page. And if your existing tiles are sound but the grout has cracked or the odd tile has gone drummy, replacing the whole floor is often the wrong call — see **[our full tiling services](/services/tiling-services)** for the repair route instead.

## FAQ

**Q1: Can you tile over a wooden floor without it cracking later?**
Yes, but not by tiling straight onto the boards. A suspended timber floor flexes, and that movement cracks rigid tile and grout. We stiffen the deck first — over-boarding and extra noggins — then bond down a decoupling membrane so the tile is separated from the timber's movement. Only then do we fix with a flexible S1 or S2 adhesive. Skipping those steps is the usual reason a timber-floor tile job fails.

**Q2: What is self-levelling compound and does my floor need it?**
Self-levelling compound is a pourable mix that flows out to correct a floor that dips or slopes. Older Dublin floors 20mm out of level are common, and that unevenness is invisible until large-format tiles start lipping — edges sitting proud of each other. We prime the sound base first, then level it; for bigger deviations we use a latex screed. The flatter the bed, the bigger the tile you can lay cleanly. We confirm what your specific floor needs at survey.

**Q3: Why do large-format tiles cost more to lay?**
Large-format tiles are less forgiving. They need a genuinely flat substrate, full back-buttering and solid-bed coverage rather than dot-and-dab, the correct notched trowel, and often levelling clips to stop lipping. That is more preparation, more time and more skill than standard porcelain. They look superb in a hallway or open-plan floor, and we are happy to fix them — the quote simply reflects the extra work. For what drives the price up or down, see our **[tiling cost factors](/cost-guide)** guide.

**Q4: What is the difference between porcelain, ceramic and natural stone?**
Porcelain is dense and barely absorbs water, so it resists frost and stains — ideal for busy floors and thresholds in the Irish climate. Ceramic is softer and more absorbent, cheaper, and well suited to walls and light-use floors. Natural stone like marble or limestone is beautiful but porous: it needs sealing, can etch and stain, and asks for more care. We talk through the trade-offs before you buy so the choice suits how the room is actually used.

**Q5: Can you tile over underfloor heating?**
Tile is the best possible finish over underfloor heating — it conducts heat well and turns a cold Dublin floor warm underfoot. Electric mats suit a single retrofit room; wet systems suit larger projects. We fix with a flexible adhesive and flexible grout, keep perimeter movement joints so the assembly can expand and contract, and heat the system up gradually per the manufacturer's instructions rather than blasting it on, which is what stresses the tile and grout.

**Q6: Do I need movement or expansion joints in a tiled floor?**
Yes, on any sizeable floor. BS 5385 best practice calls for movement joints at floor perimeters against walls, over structural joints, at changes of substrate, across large areas and around the edges of underfloor-heated zones. They let the floor expand and contract without tenting or cracking. They are easy to leave out to save time, and leaving them out is a leading cause of a floor lifting later. We plan them into the layout from the start rather than as an afterthought.

**Q7: How much does floor and wall tiling cost in Dublin?**
It depends on real factors, so we give a written quote rather than a fixed online price. What pushes it up: floors needing stiffening, levelling and decoupling; large-format or natural stone tiles, which are slower and more skilled; intricate patterns like herringbone; and awkward period-home access. What keeps it down: a sound existing substrate, standard-format porcelain and a simple straight lay. Our **[tiling cost factors](/cost-guide)** page sets out what drives the price behind them.

**Q8: Do you do bathroom and kitchen tiling too?**
We do, but they are treated as their own crafts because the details differ. Wet zones need waterproofing and tanking, covered on our **[bathroom & shower tiling](/services/bathroom-tiling-dublin)** page, and kitchen work centres on splashbacks and worktop-height detailing, covered under **[kitchen tiling & splashbacks](/services/kitchen-tiling-dublin)**. This page is for general floor and wall tiling across the home — hallways, living areas and beyond. You can see everything together under **[our full tiling services](/services/tiling-services)**.
